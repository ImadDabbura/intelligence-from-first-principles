# Michael Levin - Why Intelligence Isn't Limited To Brains.

Published: 2024-10-24 · Decision: **Core** · Confidence: high for conceptual value; moderate for the strength of the broader empirical interpretation

## Central claim

Core for my research because it expands the candidate anatomy of intelligence from brains and language to trainable, goal-maintaining systems at multiple scales. Its strongest contribution is an experimentally testable change of questions—what can this system remember, regulate and learn?—while its metaphysical and universal claims require substantial resistance.

## Ideas I want to keep

- The space in which an agent acts need not be ordinary locomotion: a bacterium can improve its situation by moving toward a nutrient or changing the genes that let it metabolize a locally available nutrient. Both can be modeled as closed-loop control over a state space.

- Gene regulatory networks are approached with conditioning protocols rather than only rewiring. Selecting neutral stimulus, unconditioned stimulus and response nodes makes habituation or association an operational hypothesis, even in a small deterministic dynamical system.

- Multiscale competency shifts engineering from specifying every component motion to changing set points and exploiting local regulation. Higher complexity can become manageable when an effective learning/control interface encapsulates it.

- Compression followed by context-sensitive reconstruction provides a proposed bridge between memory, development and adaptation. The idea is fruitful as a hypothesis about retaining task-relevant information, though compression alone does not establish intelligence.

- A collective can maintain goals that conflict with those of components. The rock-climber/skin-cell example is an intuitive corrective to assuming that membership in a larger intelligence entails shared welfare or aligned objectives.

## Mathematical anatomy

**Objects:** State variables for chemical concentrations, gene expression and morphology; stimulus histories; set points or target regions; interacting controllers at different spatial and temporal scales; compressed memory traces.

**Objective:** Operationally: preserve or attain a preferred region despite perturbation, learn from history and reuse competencies under changed conditions. The interview proposes these as dimensions of agency, not a single accepted scalar intelligence measure.

**Mechanism:** For a controlled dynamical system dx/dt=f(x,u), compare responses to paired and unpaired input histories u(t). History-dependent responses can arise through persistent state, attractor changes or slow variables without changing network topology. At a higher level, alter coupling or target conditions and test whether component behavior restores a collective pattern.

**Assumptions:** A meaningful system boundary, measurable target and appropriate timescale can be specified; apparent learning survives controls for fatigue, sensitization and residual state; higher-level descriptions predict or enable interventions better than simpler alternatives.

**Limits:** An observer-useful control description is not evidence of consciousness. Least-action formulations do not by themselves demonstrate adaptive agency. Lossy reconstruction can be entirely algorithmic; neither creativity nor noncomputability follows from compression.

## Evidence, limitations and corrections

- 30:30–37:24 describes the gene-network experiments, including six learning types and a minimal four-node network. These are author reports in an interview; protocols, controls and effect sizes are not reproduced here.

- 31:42–32:02 overstates the contrast with mechanistic modeling: dynamical-systems and control theory can explicitly study input-driven learning, memory and attractor switching without rewiring. The agential vocabulary may guide discovery, but is not required by mathematics.

- 17:52–21:56 proposes the bow-tie intelligence ratchet. Biology can overfit historical environments and fail under novelty; the transcript does not establish a universal ratchet or that decompression is nonalgorithmic.

- 43:27–47:28 carefully broadens embodiment to perception–prediction–action loops and accepts that actions must affect future perceived conditions. This is much more operational than simply calling all matter intelligent.

- 54:46–57:06 discusses bioelectric reconnection in animal cancer models. This remains a research claim, not established human treatment or a general account of all cancer. The recalled ancient cancer-lineage detail is not verified and is unnecessary to the conceptual argument.

- The values discussion explicitly identifies compassion as a chosen normative commitment (11:12–13:42), not a necessary consequence of intelligence. The host’s suggestion that evolution optimizes survival of all life should not be treated as a standard evolutionary principle.

## Objections and open questions

If agency is broadened until it includes every stationary-action trajectory, it risks explaining nothing. I would require predictive gain, controllability or generalization beyond a simpler dynamical description. Conversely, complete microphysical determinism does not make memory or learned control unreal; mechanistic and agential explanations can be compatible at different levels.

## An experiment I could try

I want to construct a four-to-six-node nonlinear ODE network with slow state, defining conditioned and unconditioned inputs and a response threshold before testing. Paired, unpaired, stimulus-only and washout controls would accompany measurements of persistence and transfer after perturbations. My question is whether a compact “learned association” model predicts held-out stimulus histories better than an equally simple nonlearning baseline. Repeating the experiment with random networks matched for dynamical stability would separate selected architecture from generic hysteresis.
